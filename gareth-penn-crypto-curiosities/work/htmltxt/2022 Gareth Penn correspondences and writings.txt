No, I haven't read Grant's book, and I don't imagine I ever will. I

published an article on this subject in Mensa Bulletin (August 1985)

and got about 500 letters in response, one from him. He and I

corresponded and talked on the phone for a while. I disagreed with him

about a theory of his, and he took offense and put me at the top of

his shit list. Years later, he accused me of being the Zodiac, finding

in my telephone number the date of Donna Lass's disappearance from

South Lake Tahoe.

There is zero evidence that the Zodiac had anything to do with Donna

Lass, and the Lake Tahoe Card text doesn't claim he did. A picture of

a housing development at Incline, Nevada, appears on a Zodiac card

mailed six months after her disappearance, and presto change-o! it

becomes a Zodiac crime. Leaving that aside, Grant finds that my phone

number in 1982-1996, 499-0670, expresses the date of Donnal Lass's

disappearance, therefore I must be the Zodiac. As I documented in

response to Grant, that number was my third wife's number, issued to

her at random by the phone company while I was still living with my

second wife. When we split in September 1982, I moved in with #3 and

eventually married her. She already had a phone, so I didn't need to

get one, and in all the 23 years I lived in Marin County, 499-0670 was

consistently listed to Diane Merrill in the Marin County directory and

never to me. I also have a formal objection: Grant takes 9/06/70 to be

the date of Donna Lass's disappearance, but in 1970, nobody wrote

dates with leading zeros. That came to us with computers years later.

In the Car Door Inscription, the Zodiac writes the date of the Blue

Rock Springs murder as "7-4-69" — no leading zero.

Further to the subject of Graysmith, you mustn't think that I despise

him because of what he wrote in his Zodiac book. I started detesting

him a dozen years earlier. Compared to his predecessor, Bob Bastian,

he was a really awful cartoonist. I talked to people at the Chronicle

who described him as a monomaniac who wandered around the house at 5th

and Mission clutching a huge bundle of papers bound with twine that he

showed to everybody and said was his Zodiac book, and he proclaimed

from time to time that it was going to be published by so-and-so, then

a year later, he'd say the same thing but with a different publisher.

St, Martin's Press was his third or fourth pubisher over a dozen

years. He also got hold of a Mensa directory and phoned up members out

of the blue to recruit them to critique his cipher solution. He was

regarded as a witless crank.

I have kept neither of Mellen's or Waltz's letters. That was 36 years

ago, and I've moved five times in the interim. But they wrote what

they wrote. Waltz was battling cancer and is probably no longer with

us, and if the same is true of Mellen, neither of them can contradict

me: I get the last word.

Ciphers ##2-4 make more sense to me as placeholders than as linguistic

texts hiding behind encryption. In any case, there is far more

information contained in the letters than in the ciphers, concealed in

the manner demonstrated in the sample I sent you.

afterthought

I should have brought this up in the 340/793 file; it waited until I

was washing the dishes to come to mind. I beg your indulgence for

abusing your patience, but it seems important. In November 1974,

Cornell astronomers Frank Drake, Carl Sagan et al. composed a message

to be transmitted by radio to putative extraterrestrials on distant

planets. The message was composed of 1679 binary bits, the product of

two prime numbers: 23 x 73.

The message as sent is a one-dimensional string of digits and is

utterly incoherent in that form. It becomes coherent when it is

formatted as a 23 W x 73L rectangle. The Zodiac provides lessons in

the literature on how to read the literarature, such as 1 + 35 = 2, 1

+ 60 = 2 (Dragon Card), and the November 1974 radio message does the

same. It begins with a demonstration of an ad hoc system off writing

base-two numbers, and those numbers are then used in the text, e.g. to

express the atomic numbers of the elements in the nucleobases of DNA.

The message is partly numerical and partly graphic. Pictorial elements

include a cross-sectional view of the DNA molecule, a similar view of

the solar system, another of the radio telescope dish from which the

message was sent, and a silhouette of the human body,

Because ETs can't be expected to read a human language, the contents

had to be nonverbal. And just as with the Zodiac letters, the message

is incoherent unless it conforms to a particular rectangular format. I

corresponded with Drake in 1985, and he was amused to find himself the

kindred spirit of a serial murderer — with respect to semiotics if not

homicide. In any case, this is one of many instances showing that as

weird as my reading of the Zodiac may seem to some people, it isn't

alll that bizarre; others have made use of the same devices. He isn't

alone.

Drake and Sagan published a detailed discussion of their radio message

in a 1975 issue of the Scientific American, if you're interested in

following up.

postscript

It occurs to me belatedly that the best way for you to validate your

solution is to submit it to peer review. Wondering whether I was just

crazy or what, I submitted an article on the Zodiac literature to

Cryptologia in 1999. It was refereed by two math PhDs who worked in

the computer security business, and they okayed it. The article was

accepted. It dealt with a whole diffferent aspect of the subject, but

the methodology was essentially the same as in the sample I sent you.

Satisfied that I wasn't out of step with objective reality, I withdrew

the submission, feeling that I had made my point. At all events,

acceptance by a refereed journal is the Good Housekeeping seal of

approval. Get that, and you could change even my jaded mind.

Sun, Mar 20, 1:23 PM 

My father was an English major employed as a newspaper writer when he

was conscripted into the U.S. Army Air Forces in the spring of 1943;

after training, he was utilized as a cryptographer at Henderson Field,

Guadalcanal. His job did not entail any original or creative work but

rather following a prescribed procedure; I don’t know what it was, but

my own military experience suggests that he used what they call an

“idiot sheet” that required him to do nothing more than fill in blanks

on a form and that the form did the en- and decryption.

His only original use of cryptic communication was the composition of

a letter to my mother some time in 1943. Soldiers were prohibited from

revealing their location in letters sent home, and outgoing mail was

subject to censorship to enforce the rule. My father sent my mother a

letter in which he praised her beauty and expressed his desire to be

reunited with her by quoting the Old Testament. The passage he chose

was from the Song of Solomon, from which she deduced that he was

stationed on Guadalcanal (in the Solomon Islands). That experience got

him interested in cryptology, and he read at least one book on the

subject (Fletcher Pratt, Secret and urgent, 1939).

After the war, he studied statistics at San Jose State College and

used that training to obtain a job with the California Department of

Highways as a statistician. He transferred from department to

department to higher-paying jobs as they became available, winding up

in the Department of Justice. The official occupying the office next

to his was the custodian of the state’s Zodiac investigation. He

described the “investigation” as a number of filing cabinets stuffed

with papers that no one looked at and hadn’t led to any actual

results, and the individual in charge of them spent his time at work

doing crossword puzzles.

Included in the state’s Zodiac files was a complete set of photographs

of the Zodiac letters. It’s hard to imagine now, but until the late

1980s, the contents of those letters were not open to public view. Few

photographic copies had been published, and passages from Zodiac texts

“quoted” in the newspapers were often edited, either by omitting

portions or by “correcting” misspellings and other seeming errors. My

father got interested in the subject, and when the custodian of the

Zodiac files looked up from one of his crossword puzzles, Dad asked

him if it was all right if he looked through the files; he got a

“Sure, go ahead. What’s a four-letter word for Greek god of war?”

One of the things that was publicly unknown was the Zodiac’s use of

the word “radian” in the July 1970 letter. When my father came across

it, he thought it incongruous. The Zodiac had been typed by

psychological profiles as a semi-moronic high school dropout. “Radian”

is a mathematical term that is unknown even to most people who take

four years of high school math (that includes me). He mentioned it to

me in the fall of 1980, and it got me interested in the Zodiac case.

That’s where it all began.

In any case, Dad got the ambition to solve the second cipher. He spent

months working on it, but was unable to produce anything but

gibberish. He finally concluded that it was unsolvable.

For my part, I went on to write an article that was published in

California magazine in November 1981 which I would disavow today as

less than half-baked, and I don’t recommend that anyone read it. In

fact, I would be embarrassed if anyone still did. But it was a

historic first: that article contained the first public mention of the

Zodiac’s use of the word “radian.” Following that, I self-published a

book titled TIMES 17, in which I presented my own solution of the

second cipher. Mine was not a verbal solution but a digital one.

Today, I would not advocate for it; I don’t believe in it any more. I

do, however, still believe that the second cipher, if it has anything

at all to say, says it with nonverbal means, i.e. I don’t credit

solutions that produce English prose.

In between the California magazine article and TIMES 17, Bob

Graysmith’s book Zodiac appeared at St. Martin’s Press (January 1986).

There, we find Graysmith’s solution of the second cipher. I won’t

quote it in full, but it contains passages such as “so stare I eat a

pill asshole” and “pardon me agcept [sic] to blast ne [sic].” Call me

crazy, but ciphers which, when “solved,” consist of nonsensical

gibberish don’t seem to me to have been solved at all. I backtracked

on Graysmith and retraced the steps he had taken. It appeared to me

that he had applied a non-rigorous methodology for turning the cipher

symbols into Roman letters which yielded a 340-letter anagram, and

then shuffled letters around to produce phraseology that he found

pleasing, e.g. “pardon me agcept to blast ne.” He claims that his

“solution” was endorsed by Greg Mellen, a founding editor of

Cryptologia, and by Eugene Waltz, the head of the American Crypogram

Association. Incredulous, I wrote to both of these individuals, and to

my utter astonishment, they confirmed that they agreed with

Graysmith’s solution. Mellen went so far as to write that he felt that

Graysmith’s solution would lead to a swift resolution of the Zodiac

case. I am no cryptologist, but I know how to count, and I note that

thirty-six years have elapsed since then without a resolution of the

Zodiac case.

Since then, a number of solutions have been published, all

contradicting Graysmith and one another as well. Several of these

solutions “prove” that I am the Zodiac. About a dozen years ago, I got

a phone call from a lady in San Rafael, California, breathlessly

informing me that “the 340” had just been solved, and it proved that

Arthur Leigh Allen was the Zodiac (Allen was Vallejo PD’s and

Graysmith’s favorite suspect). She was giddy with excitement, and I

didn’t have the heart to point out to her that a DNA comparison

carried out in 2002 had ruled Allen out as a Zodiac suspect.

I haven’t read any solutions since 1986, being jaundiced on that

subject. I couldn’t avoid finding out about the recent solution, by a

French analyst, of the third and fourth ciphers — someone emailed the

contents to me — which gives the latitude and longitude of a point in

the Gulf of Saint Lawrence, in the open ocean between Nova Scotia and

Newfoundland, and somehow connects this with Lake Tahoe, an alpine

lake in the Sierra Nevada, 3000 miles away. Maybe I’m just not smart

enough to see how this relates to the Zodiac case, but I fail to see

the connection. An Australian analyst finds that the three Arabic 8s

in the third cipher correspond to the eight weeks of Basic Combat

Training I underwent at Fort Dix, New Jersey, the eight weeks of

Advanced Individual Training I took at Fort Sill, Oklahoma, and an

eight-week training course in how to be an instructor, also at Fort

Sill. The only problem with this reading is that following AIT, I not

only didn’t take an instructor-training course (there wasn’t one) but

OJT’d as a clerk; the balance of my military service was spent working

as a battery clerk with the official title of “personnel specialist.”

This same creative individual suggests that I must be the Zodiac

because I was admitted to the US at McGuire AFB by the Immigration and

Naturalization Service (now ICE) on 30 October 1965, a year to the day

before the Riverside murder, because it is part of my lifestyle to

celebrate the anniversary of arriving at a U.S. Air Force base by

murdering someone. I mean, doesn’t everybody?

But I digress. I don’t hang out at Zodiac websites trading

inside-baseball banter with Zodiologists, because I find that I don’t

learn anything useful by doing so. And when I am forced into

interacting with these people, they insinuate that I am a serial

murderer. (I wrote a book about the Zodiac, therefore I must be the

Zodiac. I mean, Columbus discovered America; therefore he must have

put it there. Well, duh!) When this happens on Twitter, I block them.

At any rate, as I think the sample I sent you makes clear, I don’t

believe in verbal solutions at all; my firm position, hardened by 41

years of work, is that anything useful concealed in the Zodiac texts

will be found using nonverbal means.

An author named Michael O’Hare published an article in the February

1964 issue of a Harvard student magazine on the subject of graphic

arts education, in which he discusses art as a nonverbal form of

expression, singling out a new art form that he calls “meta-writing,”

a form of writing in which the graphic form of the writing is more

interesting than the verbal content of the text (I think that’s

verbatim). I believe he would agree with me that the best way to deal

with the Zodiac problem is to treat Zodiac texts as exemplars of

“meta-writing.”

Gareth
