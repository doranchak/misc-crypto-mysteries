This blog entry will make the point that Gareth Penn’s “manifesto”, Appendix A of Times 17, was written entirely for the sake of providing a plausible context for its last line–

I went looking for the Zodiac, but what I found was the roots of fascism.

–which has a real meaning very different from its contextual one.

In doing this, I’m going to take the reader back more than 23 years to my state of mind when I was writing the initial edition of TZMS, January-August 1990.  Zodiac message board posters don’t get what Gareth Penn is doing, simply because they don’t understand Gareth Penn.  The small handful of investigators who have interacted with Penn over the past decade or so–including Mike Butterfield and Mike Martin–have misunderstood Penn, period.

I’ve already cited the passage from Hamlet where the king’s advisor, Polonius, is trying to figure out whether Hamlet is mad or sane.  It’s clear that Hamlet is playing with Polonius, who isn’t smart enough to figure out that he’s being mocked, and yet, Polonius is smart enough to realize that there’s meaning in Hamlet’s words, hence: “Though this be madness, yet there is method in’t.”

I will begin by noting that the chapter which immediately precedes Appendix A is itself called “Times 17″, and has mostly to do with Gareth Penn constructing a magic square, 17 by 17, where the numbers in each column and row add up to 9745.  Gareth clearly thought enough of this exercise that he put the result on the cover of his book.

 [LINK http://raygranttzms.files.wordpress.com/2013/12/times-17.png] 

It’s clear to everyone, except perhaps the Z Crypto Think Tank, that the Zodiac Killer was preoccupied with the number 17.

There were 17 mailings during the Zodiac’s initial Bay Area period:

1. The Cryptogram letter to The Vallejo Times-Herald (July 31, 1969)

2. The Cryptogram letter to The San Francisco Examiner (July 31, 1969)

3. The Cryptogram letter to The San Francisco Chronicle (July 31, 1969)

4. The “This is the Zodiac speaking” Letter (August 7, 1969)

5. The School Bus Threat (October 13, 1969)

6. The Pen Card (November 8, 1969)

7. The Bus Bomb Diagram (November 9, 1969)

8. The “Dear Melvin” Letter (December 20, 1969)

9. The 13-Character Cipher (April 20, 1970)

10. The Little Dragon Card (April 28, 1970)

11. The Mt. Diablo Letter (June 26, 1970)

12. The Kidnapping Confirmation (July 24, 1970)

13. The Mikado Letter (July 26, 1970)

15. The Halloween Card (October 27, 1970)

Three of the Zodiac’s four ciphers feature lines of symbols totaling 17.  Even the 32-Character Cipher at the bottom of The Mt. Diablo Letter of June 26, 1970 features a first line of 17 followed by a line of 15, rather than two lines of 16.

 [LINK http://raygranttzms.files.wordpress.com/2013/12/mt-diablo-letter.png] 

How likely is it that Gareth Penn chose Michael O’Hare (DOB 1-22-43) purely by chance, and that O’Hare’s age in days (9,745) on a Zodiac murder date (Lake Berryessa, September 27, 1969) when the killer stabbed his victims 17 times, just happens to convert to TIMES 17?

T         I           M         E          S          17

1          00       11       0          000     10001 = 9745 (digital Morse/binary math)

Clearly, Gareth considered this insight of prime importance in his assessment of the Zodiac murders; he used it as the title of his book.  A killer who appears to be obsessed with the number 17 (even though The Cryptogram “solution” doesn’t fit its format, going 18 letters over) just happens to stab two victims that number of times (even though he was free to stab them all afternoon if he wanted, and seems to have gone to a lot of trouble just to stab them each for a matter of seconds), and a phrase expressing that number converts to his age in days on the date the attack took place.

I’ve pointed out that the Zodiac mailed The LA Times Letter on March 15, 1971.  The LA Times Letter is the 16th mailing during the Zodiac’s Bay Area period.  The Zodiac abruptly breaks off his one way correspondence with The SF Chronicle to do his single mailing to The LA Times.  The letter is significant because the Zodiac’s ever-ascending scoreboard number has now reached 17.  With the name of the paper—never written to before, and never written to afterward—it expresses the phrase, “LA Times 17”, or, more to the point, “Times 17”.  If he writes that letter to the San Francisco Chronicle, he can’t make that point.

So The LA Times Letter contains a form of the expression, “Times 17″.

One week later, on March 22, 1971, the Zodiac mails The Lake Tahoe Card, which includes a punched hole in the upper right corner.  Police agencies connected with the Zodiac case immediately assumed the punched hole signified the burial place of missing Lake Tahoe nurse, Donna Lass.  They began digging holes in the vicinity of the Forest Pines condominium, to no avail.  Still, it’s interesting that police agencies, always quick to dismiss any “cryptographic” angle on the Zodiac case, took such a “cryptographic” approach to what was on the card.

 [LINK http://raygranttzms.files.wordpress.com/2013/12/lake-tahoe-card.png] 

The punched hole represents, in fact, a circle, the center of which is an x/y coordinate.  The center of the hole, measured from the left edge of the card—the x coordinate—is exactly 97mm.  The center of the hole, measured from the bottom of the card–the y coordinate–is exactly 45mm.  The punched hole, as an x/y coordinate, is 97/45.

So the final two Zodiac mailings in The Bay Area, mailings #16 and #17, represent the expressions “Times 17” and “9745”.

T         I           M         E          S          17

1          00       11       0          000     10001  =  9745 (digital Morse/binary math)

Since TIMES 17 = 9745 in Morse code/binary math, it’s hard for me to see that as a coincidence.  And those two examples aren’t from Gareth Penn’s book; they’re from the Zodiac Killer’s letters.

In the article immediately preceding Appendex A, Gareth segues from his discussion of magic squares to an assessment of how modern society deals with criminals.  Because our industry and agriculture has become mechanized, we have the leisure time to set up a criminal justice system with police, courts, and prisons.  Gareth points out that more primitive societies dealt with criminals by exiling them, shunning them, and in some instances even murdering them.  Gareth seems to be saying that the Zodiac case requires some primordial option that isn’t available any more, because we’ve evolved beyond it.

It’s almost as if Gareth is saying that this case is beyond the reach of conventional law enforcement, as if it belongs in another category that can’t be processed by what modern civilization has set up to deal with such behavior . . . because this particular type of behavior doesn’t fit any category that’s been imagined so far.

After pointing out all the risks he’s had to take to bring this information to the reader, he then asks the reader for an objective assessment of his work.

One might have expected the book to end right there, but it doesn’t.

I went to the trouble of retyping Appendix A from Times 17.  As I’ve pointed out, I typed professionally for short stints during my employment career, and am fast enough to type live closed captioning (though, granted, no one does that well).  Typing out the appendix still took me the greater part of a morning.

APPENDIX A

WHAT WENT WRONG?

In the summer of 1986, I sent a ZIK to a correspondent in Southern California.  Just a few weeks before, I had mentioned the name of Michael O’Hare to him in connection with the Zodiac murders, and his first reaction was to call Mike up and tell him that I had suggested that he was a criminal.  Mike’s response, incidentally, was to say that he couldn’t sue me because he would have to prove damages.  [NB: The law specifically exempts plaintiffs in defamation cases from having to prove special damages.  His excuse for not suing me simply didn’t hold water.  But he did have to say something, and doubletalk was better than nothing.]

A ZIK was a Zodiac Identification Kit, a mailing whose contents identified Michael O’Hare as the Zodiac Killer.

I’m not a lawyer, but as I understand it, the difference between special and general damages is that special damages (medical bills, loss of property) have a specific monetary amount attached to them, where general damages (mental anguish, pain and suffering) do not.  Gareth is saying that a plaintiff doesn’t have to point to any specific loss (for example, losing one’s job, which happened to Michael O’Hare as a result of my own accusation), that insult is sufficient without injury.  My own understanding is that most jurisdictions presume public accusation of a felony to be damaging.  The notion that being a public figure leaves you open for defamation is ludicrous; Kjell Qvale is infinitely more famous than Michael O’Hare, and yet, Qvale had no trouble shutting down Mike Rodelli’s website the instant he was named on it.

At all events, I then zicked my correspondent.  A few days later, he called to exclaim, after having seen the handwriting comparisons, “How has he [i. e., Mike O’Hare] managed to stay out of jail all these years?”  He found the corroborative evidence overwhelming.  If your reaction to the evidence presented in this book is like that of everyone I have talked to who has taken five minutes to look at it, you may be asking yourself the same thing.

To be ‘zicked’ means being the recipient of a ZIK.

This paragraph is somewhat misleading, in the sense that a very high percentage of Times 17 first edition copies–or perhaps I should say, copies that were mailed to book buyers by Gareth Penn himself–went to Mensans, which is to say, they went to people with IQs in the upper 2% of the population.

I don’t know if the reader has noticed, but most people who post on Zodiac message boards do not have IQs in the upper 2% of the population.  In 1987, most of the people outside of law enforcement that Gareth had talked to about the Zodiac case were Mensans, so the reaction he got to the evidence presented in the book was not reflective of society at large.  Most Zodiac message board posters are people of average intelligence, at best.  Relatively few of them can piece a handful of grammatical sentences together, much less a couple thoughts together.  Since the Zodiac messages contained poor grammar and bad spelling, one is tempted to believe that that’s what attracted them to the case in the first place.  They felt a semi-literate kinship with the writer of those letters.

He raised an interesting question.  How has the Zodiac managed to evade arrest and prosecution for the last twenty years?  Quite apart from the matter of justice, if all the foregoing is worth anything at all, it ought to contain some kind of lesson or generalization which we can apply to other situations.  As this is a free country, you can draw your own lessons from it.  As this is my forum, I am going to suggest some for you to think about.

I don’t think the failure of law enforcement to deal with this criminal is due to his manifestly superior intelligence.  It is due to major failings of our society, of which I would like to pinpoint three.

Before we get into Gareth’s specific examples, I think it’s fair to suggest that law enforcement did an imperfect job of processing the Zodiac crimes, mostly because the crimes themselves were stage-managed to look as conventional as possible.  There was nothing about the Cheri Bates murder or the Lake Herman Road murders that made them appear to be major mysteries at the time they were committed, though there were multiple aspects of both murder events that didn’t make sense.  It is difficult to gauge what Riverside PD’s “take” on the Cheri Bates murder would have been had The Confession Letter not been sent, since The CL is the source for the spurned suitor theory.  It’s clear that Solano County Sheriff’s Office believed the Lake Herman Road murders to be drug-related, though nothing about the crime scene itself points in that direction; they just couldn’t explain why two teenagers would be gunned down execution style for no apparent reason (especially since the girl wasn’t molested, in contrast to Domingos/Edwards).

The first failing is a widespread and profound contempt for humanistic education.  This contempt is particularly prominent in law enforcement, but it has not been patented by the police.  I don’t want to suggest that police detectives should take courses in medieval Provencal love poetry.  Obviously, training in literature or linguistics seldom has any bearing on the detection of crimes.  Policemen require training in analytical methods, but it doesn’t have to consist of parsing Homeric hexameters.

My experience with the police over the last six years suggests to me that they are convinced that literary analysis is unscientific.  This seems to me to be a counterproductive assumption, since almost all of the evidence in this particular case is literary.  The Zodiac left very little physical evidence, and the eyewitness evidence, while apparently good, has advanced the case to nowhere between 11 October 1969 and the present.  But we do have a large quantity of the murderer’s writings, and it seems to me that a literary-analytical approach ought to have been the first resort, not the last.

Clearly, I disagree with Gareth about the evidence.  There is, in fact, a tremendous amount of conventional evidence which has not been carefully examined in any of the Zodiac murders.  As I’ve demonstrated so far for Riverside, Lake Herman Road, and Presidio Heights, the true crime narrative of those events is dead wrong:

1.  Cheri Bates never made it to the RCC Library the night of her murder.  She was abducted circa 6:20pm.  Her stomach contents tell us she was in fear of her life for a protracted period, several hours, that evening.  She was murdered by at least two people, because the epicenter of her wounds was her throat, which was slashed deeply and efficiently at least 7 times.  All her other wounds are superficial, and she has no wounds whatsoever below her breasts.  That wound pattern could not possibly be produced by a spurned suitor lunging with a knife in a dark alley.  It is consistent with two men wrestling her to the ground, the man behind her slashing her throat while the man in front of her pinned her arms to her sides.

2.  The two Lake Herman Road victims could not possibly have been killed by a single gunman, because David Faraday was killed by a contact wound to his left ear, and the first shot that hit Betty Lou Jensen, the uppermost shot on her back, contained a single grain of GSR.  That is consistent with her being about 5 feet from the gun barrel when the shot was fired.  If she’s closer, there’s more GSR; if she’s farther than that, there’s no GSR at all.  The only way a lone gunman could possibly produce that effect is if he held Betty Lou by the arm while he was shooting David in the left side of the head.  But if that’s how he killed the couple, why were two shots fired into the car?  And if he herded the couple out the passenger side by firing into the car, how could he possibly have been close enough to Betty Lou to hit her within 5 feet, AFTER shooting David in the side of the head, since she would have exited the car first?  If he pauses after circling the car to shoot David at point blank range, a contact wound, Betty Lou is also much farther away from the car when she falls after being hit 5 times, and in that case, she wouldn’t have fallen backward.

3.  The Zodiac Killer was sitting in the back of the cab when he shot Paul Stine.  We know this because Stine’s bloodied map fell into the street when the morgue stewards were pulling the victim’s body out of the car.  That means the map was on the front passenger seat, which means the Zodiac wasn’t sitting there during the trip.  The Zodiac shot Paul Stine immediately after Stine put the cab in PARK.  We know this because the meter was still running when Detectives Toschi and Armstrong arrived at the cab.  That means the killer shot Stine in the brief moment between Stine putting the cab in PARK and shutting off the meter.  That means the killer had planned to kill the victim the moment he put the cab in PARK.  But we also know the killer shot Stine in front of and above the right ear, which means he had to hunch forward and point the gun perpendicular to the side of Stine’s head before shooting.

He shot Stine sideways through the head, from right-to-left, with a slightly forward angle.  This shot is a mirror image of the shot he fired through David Faraday’s head, also a contact wound, but in the left side of Faraday’s head, from left-to-right with a slightly forward angle.  There is, in other words, every reason to believe that the unusual angle of the two shots was intentional, that the first public victim and the last public victim were intended to bracket each other, both figuratively and literally.  Among other things, it suggests that the killer is responsible for other, non-bracketed, murders.

The second major defect in our system is the press, or to use a more modern term, the media.  In the ideal Jeffersonian universe, the media act as a corrective to the faults of government.  My experience has taught me that the media in fact amplify those faults.  The function of the free press is supposed to be rooting out corruption.  But how can it perform this task when it is fundamentally corrupt itself?

I am not suggesting that reporters take bribes in the form of cash payments (although that seems to happen from time to time).  The bribes which the media do take are increases in circulation and advertising revenue.  In the interest of keeping those hefty revenues flowing, newspapers, magazines, and electronic media outlets pander to the lowest common denominator, doing everything they can to titillate the public’s desire for sensational stories, and doing violence to the truth wherever needful in the interest of sensationalism.

In the particular case of the Zodiac, false reporting of the linguistic facts, not to mention imaginative interpretations of Zodiac utterances, has characterized media coverage, especially that of the San Francisco Chronicle, which is surely one of the ethically and intellectually most corrupt newspapers in the world.  I believe that this judgment is sufficiently supported by my experience, on which I have reported here.

I’d better comment on this section now before Gareth launches into his long-winded example of media corruption.  As I just pointed out, any issues with reporting on the Zodiac case pretty much begins and ends with the investigative journalism of the crimes themselves.  Since only a handful of killers has written to the newspapers, and none of those non-Zodiac messages approaches the complexity that Gareth is claiming for the Zodiac letters, it would be difficult to hold the media responsible for interpreting something that, by his own admission, was unprecedented.  On the other hand, reporters for the Riverside Press and the Riverside Enterprise can be criticized for not probing the RCC Library reconstruction more than they did.  How could someone as popular and outgoing and beautiful as Cheri Bates was go unnoticed inside a 2400 square foot building for 2 hours and 40 minutes?   Reporters for the Vallejo Times-Herald can be faulted for not chronicling the movements of vehicles on Lake Herman Road in greater detail, and in particular for not focusing more on the mysterious white Chevy Impala that was not connected to anyone in the neighborhood, but which was parked out in the middle of nowhere on a cold night for almost an hour without any occupants inside it.  Reporters for The San Francisco Chronicle and The San Francisco Examiner can be questioned for not nailing down the disconnect between the SFPD’s version of events and the Zodiac’s.

The Zodiac was perfectly aware of the Chronicle’s penchant for sensationalism (all he had to do was read a few issues to see it), and I suggest that that was a major factor in his selection of this newspaper as the recipient of most of his letters.  He knew how it would be interpreted if he were to write a number at the foot of a letter—the Chronicle would literally squeeze blood out of a turnip if it could, on the theory that gore sells newspapers.  And so he played on the newspaper the way that Yitzhak Perlman plays on his fiddle.  The one major journalistic contribution to the solution of the Zodiac puzzle, the revelation of the Riverside murder, sold newspapers in November 1970; it took the Chronicle sixteen years to figure out how to sell newspapers by repudiating its own scoop, but eventually they managed (see below).

This presumes that big city newspapers are necessarily monolithic in their coverage of major stories, which they aren’t.  Just to cite one example of the opposite in the Zodiac case itself, you had the Armistead Maupin (Tales of the City) feud with the Robert Graysmith/Dave Toschi faction when the April 1978 letter was being investigated as a possible fake by SFPD.  The Riverside link scoop was Paul Avery’s, but it’s a bit much to suggest, in a story as big as the Zodiac murders, that that story reflected the Chronicle’s editorial view, especially when Riverside PD denied any link to the Zodiac case.

I have suggested that the most important evidence relating to the Zodiac’s identity is his literary legacy.  How can that legacy be evaluated intelligently when it has been trivialized and bowdlerized by theChronicle?  How can anyone form an intelligent opinion about the facts, when the Chronicle consistently reports them wrong?  Obviously, the student must resort to other sources of information.

I was about to ask for an example; Gareth provides one.

An excellent example of this kind of intellectual corruption at work is found in the Chronicle story by Sandra Hansen Konte, which I mentioned elsewhere.  Besides willfully and egregiously misquoting me—for the sake of a better story—she leads into her subject with this passage:

Eighteen years ago yesterday [20 December 1986], the Zodiac killer murdered his first victim.  He has not been heard from since 1974.  So why do we keep thinking about him?

In a two-part series run under Paul Avery’s by-line on 16 and 17 November 1970, the Chronicle had revealed for the first time ever that the Zodiac had murdered Cheri Jo Bates in Riverside on 30 October 1966.  As far as anybody knew between 1970 and 1986, she was his first victim.  But now in December 1986, Konte—and the Chronicle—decided to overlook the Riverside murder.  Why?

Since I have a writing degree and Gareth doesn’t, allow me to make a suggestion.  If the paper’s editors considered this a big deal, which clearly they didn’t, they could have sidestepped Gareth’s objections simply by tweaking the passage as follows:

Eighteen years ago yesterday [20 December 1986], the Zodiac killer murdered his first two victims in the San Francisco Bay Area.  He has not been heard from since 1974.  So why do we keep thinking about him?

Easy peazy.

I confess that I was not present and that the following is just a reconstruction based on my knowledge of media practice.  The Konte story was intended for the Sunday magazine section This World (where it in fact appeared).  There was no issue open until December.  One Sunday in December, the 21st, happened to follow the eighteenth anniversary of the Lake Herman Road murders by twenty-four hours.  Editors like to have a “hook” on which to hang feature stories; anniversaries are very good hooks to use.

The Sunday closest to the twentieth anniversary of the Riverside murder was not available, since the magazine’s contents for that date had already been determined.

In 1986, October 30 was on a Thursday, so any “hook” made to the Riverside murder in a Sunday magazine section would have been awkward for two reasons:

1.  The date link would either have been 4 days in the future or 3 days in the past;

2.  The link would refer to an event in Riverside, not San Francisco.  City newspapers are always looking to cite a local link to any story, even one so closely associated to San Francisco as the Zodiac Killer.

Twenty years ago this past Thursday, the Zodiac killer murdered his first victim in Riverside.  He has not been heard from since 1974.  So why do we keep thinking about him?

See what I mean?

So the story was put into the issue for 21 December, and to make the “hook” more sensational, the Riverside murder, which the Chronicle itself had originally attributed to the Zodiac—and which Z. had admitted to following publication of Avery’s story in 1970—was downgraded.  In her article, Konte says that the attribution of the Bates murder to Z. is only a “theory”.

This reflects the viewpoint of RIverside PD.  I disagree with that viewpoint, but since the police agency with jurisdiction in the Bates case is Riverside PD, and they believe the Zodiac link is only a theory, it’s hard to fault a reporter for citing that viewpoint.  To repeat what I’ve said any number of times now, Riverside PD was initially enthusiastic about the Zodiac link (see the October 20, 1969 memo from Chief Kinkead to Napa Sheriff’s Office) pending the imminent comparison between the latent prints from the VW of Cheri Bates and SFPD’s prints from Presidio Heights.  Since they did an almost immediate about-face and denied any link between their homicide and the Zodiac, at the very least by the time of Paul Avery’s story in November 1970 (see the David Fincher film where this is dramatized), we can only conclude that the prints were mutually exclusive.  That eliminates the Zodiac if one person killed Cheri Bates, and if the Zodiac was only one person.

But we know, from the Cheri Bates autopsy, that she was killed by at least two people.  And we know, from the ballistic evidence at Lake Herman Road, that David Faraday and Betty Lou Jensen were executed by at least two people (consistent with the number of occupants seen inside the Chevy Impala).

That makes Lake Herman Road the “first” Zodiac murder, which justifies her lead-in, and that in turn makes her story more sensational.  The truth is thus subordinated to the economics of peddling theChronicle, and the facts are so compromised that no one could base a sensible judgment on them in the form in which Konte “reports” (i. e., distorts) them.

Lake Herman Road was the first Bay Area Zodiac murder event, which, frankly, is what most readers of the Chronicle would be interested in.  If the reporter in question led the story with a Riverside link, a lot of its potential readers would have stopped reading and moved on to the funny pages or the editorial cartoons.  The truth to be found on the pages of any newspaper is always subordinated to the economics of peddling that newspaper, to the extent that, if the paper loses enough readers that it stops making money, there’s no paper to read.

The third failing is in the general reliance in American society today on psychological speculation.  Everybody and his brother is an armchair psychologist, and I have never talked to anyone about the Zodiac without the conversation beginning with psychological generalizations.  Some years back, a very useful book was published under the title Psychobabble.  I gather that it sold rather well, but it seems to have had no lasting power.  Its thesis was that factual and logical discourse has been completely replaced by jargon borrowed eclectically from the field of psychology and which is generally not understood even by the person who is using it.

Most Zodiac message board posters believe the Zodiac Killer is a slobbering psychotic.  No psychotic, however presentable, could possibly have committed the Zodiac murders, since those murders required a logistical presence of mind that a psychotic isn’t capable of.

It has been my experience over the last several years that people who use psychobabble are doing so in order to get around difficult intellectual and moral choices.  If you don’t understand something, discuss it in terms of complexes or psychopathology.  Since what you are saying can neither be proven nor disproven, you are safe in saying whatever you want.

Again, I disagree.  David Van Nuys has said that the Zodiac Killer suffered from Dissociative Identity Disorder.  If this were true, there would be clear partitioning between his various personalities which would be apparent in his letters.  Instead, the Zodiac is clearly aware of everything he’s done, and even openly mocks the recipients of his letters with his knowledge of their motives.  For example, Melvin Belli attempted to manipulate “Sam”, the mental patient who called the Channel 7 Show, by suggesting he could help him with his headaches.  The Zodiac Killer responded by sending a letter to his home address, a letter filled with remorse and regret for what his condition was forcing him to do.  Of course, the Zodiac was just “goofing” on Belli; his next letters went back to his usual menacing tone.

The application of psychology to criminal detection has produced the psychological profile, an instrument which, by the FBI’s own admission, is less accurate than flipping a coin.  Yet FBI agents thump on their profiles as if Moses had toted them down from the summit of Sinai.  And for twenty years, anyone like Mike O’Hare has been excluded from consideration as a suspect because of the pontifications of law enforcement’s psychological gurus.

Profiling may have some rudimentary value, in the sense that most serial killers may strike first in locations where they feel comfortable.  The more specific the profile attempts to become, the less reliable it tends to be.  No psychologist in his right mind ever attempts a diagnosis of a patient he hasn’t examined in a clinical environment.  A more traditional approach–for example, that most victims know their killers–yields a much higher hit rate.

Psychological jargon is also favored as a means of obfuscating moral issues.  As a society, we tend to shy away from moral judgments, perhaps because they seem old-fashioned.  Since nature abhors a vacuum, when matters can not be discussed in polite company in moral terms, psychological terminology rushes in to fill the gap.  And the advantage of psychological jargon is that it can be bent to mean anything.

California magazine subtitled my Zodiac article as “A Tale of Obsession.”  Vic Fershko, trying to make me out an unfit parent, called me “obsessive” in court because of the time that I was devoting to this project.  Sandra Konte fabricated a quote for her Chronicle article in which I “cheerfully” confessed to being “obsessive.”  If you went by what all of these self-diploma’d psychiatrists have put in the public record, Gareth Penn is practically a raving maniac.

Gareth cites two examples, one a court tactic from a divorce attorney, the other an offhanded remark in a Sunday magazine piece about a serial murder case.

By the end of my fifth year in this project, I had heard this theme enough to make me wonder myself.  At that time, I was in the process of receiving hundreds of letters from Mensans all over the United States in response to the publication of the Mensa Bulletin article.  Quite a few of them made use of words like “persistence,” “tenacity,” and “courage” to describe what Drs. Bates, Fershko, and Konte describe as “obsession.”  In other words, what some people see as virtues, others see as symptoms of psychopathology.  What our grandparents thought of as positive moral values, we think of as manifestations of mental instability.

If there’s a thin line between love and hate, there’s a much thinner one between obsession and due diligence.

I have already mentioned that the only real psychiatrist with whom I have ever spent much time discussing this matter, Leonti Thompson, suggested to me that the Zodiac’s mental problem is that he is simply “evil.”  It was very refreshing to me to hear a statement couched in moral terms coming from the lips of someone with genuine credentials in psychiatry.  Be that as it may, psychobabble not only turns virtue into sickness; it also excuses evil, the opposite of virtue, as a medical problem.  Children are excused from school attendance if they have a note from Mommy saying that they have been ill.  To a large degree, we tend to excuse immoral behavior in adults on the same grounds.

I’m guessing that if Berta Margoulies were alive, she’d be excusing her Zodiac Project as confrontational art taken to its logical extreme.

I can’t count the number of people who have told me that the Zodiac, like all criminals of his type (is there anyone else like him?), is “insane.”  How do we know this?  Because only an insane person would do something like this.  Noses were made in just the right way to support eyeglasses, which proves that this is the best of all possible worlds.  QED.

Fortunately, the Zodiac was content with executing a conceptual art project.  That required only a handful of homicides.  What if his objective had been to overthrow the United States Government?  Given his superior capabilities and the manifest inability of the forces of law and order to deal with him, it seems likely that he or anyone like him would have a fairly easy time of it.  And for all we know, our political and economic institutions have already been undermined by individuals of high mental capacity, who are simply not being apprehended or prosecuted.

It does seem clear that our political and economic institutions are being controlled by a relative handful of individuals who are immune to democracy.  Instant background checks for gun purchasers is something everyone’s in favor of–Democrats, Independents, Republicans, and even National Rifle Association members–but, even in the wake of the Sandy Hook massacre, it had no chance of passing.

What stands between us and chaos?  We have abandoned moral values, which are meant to serve as guidelines for our own behavior and against which to measure the behavior of others.  We have no respect for humanistic intellectual tools—except insofar as they keep us supplied with electronic gadgetry—tools which help us to analyze difficult or complex problems.  And the institution of the press, which is supposed to act as an honest broker of information, has been turned into the exact opposite of that by its own economic imperatives.

Again, citing a Sunday magazine article about the Zodiac murders to suggest that civilization is crumbling seems a bit much.

The laws of probability dictate that eventually that string of luck will break.  What will we do then?  How will we deal with the resulting horrors without intellectual integrity or moral values?  To see the consequences of the abandonment of values, all you have to do is look back to the history of Central Europe between 1935 and 1945.  All the same ingredients are with us here and now.

What ARE the roots of fascism, anyway?

Let’s pull out our Morse/binary conversion table and see!

So what, you ask?

Why would Gareth go to that much trouble just to make that statement?

It’s because he’s suggesting that that same operation be performed on two other words that are featured prominently in the Zodiac case: PUMPKIN and DRAGON.

ZKF has a thread on its board with an ironic title: Penn Debunked For Dummies.  The reason the thread’s title is unintentionally ironic is that there are actually TWO Gareth Penns:

There’s the “face value” Gareth, and there’s the “real” Gareth.

Everyone, and I mean everyone, in the Zodiac online community believes in the “face value” Gareth, with a single exception (that would be me).  Even the people who dismiss everything he says still believe in the “face value” Gareth.  Even the posters who tried to shut down the Gareth Penn thread on ZKS (Tahoe27 and her henchwomen) still believe in the “face value” Gareth.  Even very smart people like Jake Wark and Mike Martin still believe in the “face value” Gareth.  But who is this “face value” Gareth I keep talking about?

The “face value” version of Gareth is slightly complicated to explain, so I’ll use a professional wrestling metaphor to make it easier for people.  Gareth, as presented in his various writings, and in his dealings with people like Mike Butterfield and Mike Martin, can be imagined as a professional wrestler who arrives on the scene and seems, in his initial appearances, to be a “good guy”.  In his first few matches, the crowd cheers, and he smiles at the crowd appreciatively, but there’s something almost too . . . vanilla about him.  He’s a little too good, and he’s a little too pleased about how squeaky clean he is.  As the weeks roll by, the crowd begins to notice that the “good guy” is sneaky, that he uses underhanded tactics when the referee’s back is turned, and that he says one thing and does something else.  Worst of all, he’s arrogant, sanctimonious, and talks too much.  Eventually, the crowd starts booing him, and starts cheering for his antagonist (Michael O’Hare, also the “face value” version), and the crowd even congratulates itself on having seen through his act.

Of course, the crowd hasn’t seen through his act at all.

Pro wrestling is fake.  Everything you see on a WWE show is fake, except for the occasional unscripted blooper (Owen Hart falling several stories to his death when the cable line dropping him into the ring during a pay-per-view malfunctions; Jerry “The King” Lawler having an actual heart attack on camera).  The “good guy” and the “bad guy” are actually on the same team, and the whole point of their interaction is to manipulate your emotions the same way a scripted TV drama does.

Very few adults believe in Santa Claus.  At some point around age 7 or so, a child realizes that no one, not even a man who had flying reindeer to tow his sleigh through the wintry night, could visit all the rooftops and chimneys in the world, or even in one’s immediate neighborhood.  No one’s stomach could absorb all those glasses of milk and plates of cookies.

Some adults take pro wrestling at face value; they’re called “marks”.  Wrestling fans who know they’re watching a scripted event are called “smarks”, and they often respond counter to a given storyline, booing the “good guy” or yelling, “Boring!” if a given wrestler’s “gimmick” is lame.

Times 17 is like a pro wrestling pay-per-view.  If you were there in 1987, like I was, you got into the show for $17 (yes, that was the original price of Times 17, tax included).  If you want a ticket nowadays, you have to pay the scalper’s rate, which is a hundred dollars or more.

But the important thing to remember is that the “good guy” and the “bad guy” are both on the same team.  And it’s not as if some Zodiac message board posters haven’t suspected this; a handful of posters on Tom’s original archived message board [LINK http://www.zodiackiller.com/mba/ozs/99.html] pointed to odd behavior on the part of Gareth Penn and Michael O’Hare, but somehow they just couldn’t put their fingers on it.

More recently, we have the example of Michael O’Hare’s Washington Monthly article, linked to ad nauseum by Mike Butterfield and even proudly copied verbatim on Butterfield’s website.  There are lots of odd things about O’Hare’s article, but I’ll cite two for the moment.

1.  A man who professes not to have time even to read two books which accused him of being the Zodiac Killer, one of which drove him from his job at the JFK School of Government at Harvard, did have time to write an article about his experience being accused by the other book writer.

2.  A man who had spent 29 years (1980-2009) accusing Michael O’Hare of being the Zodiac Killer then chose not to sue O’Hare after he was held up to ridicule, even though suing him would result in his obtaining O’Hare’s fingerprints, which would determine once and for all if O’Hare was the Zodiac Killer.

Of course, if these two are really on the same team, their actions make sense.  The purpose of their “face value” interaction, played out for the readers of a national opinion magazine, is to further the fiction that they’re antagonists.

Here’s a question: if Michael O’Hare and Gareth Penn were actually the Zodiac Killers, how would they behave toward a REAL antagonist?

Here are a few examples:

1.  If you were Michael O’Hare, and a real antagonist like Ray Grant accused you of being the Zodiac Killer in front of your colleagues at your place of business, you wouldn’t sue him.  If Harvard asked you to allow them to sue Mr. Grant on your behalf, you would respond by quitting your job, selling your house, and moving yourself, your wife, your two little girls, and your elderly mother to the other end of the country.  You would do this even if Mr. Grant accused your mother of being complicit in the serial murders he was accusing you of committing.  After all, if you’re already committing career suicide and costing your wife her state government job and forcing your kids to make new friends in a new school district, why bother defending your mother’s reputation?

2.  If you were Gareth Penn, you would not confront Ray Grant directly.  Instead, you would get intermediates to post your Q&A on their various message boards.  You would also post whatever remarks you wanted to make on a blog that doesn’t feature your name anywhere.  That way, when Ray tries to sue you, no one’s home, and the address is just a facade with a picture propped up like a cardboard person.  It’s the same principle as defendants in civil cases moving all their money into accounts with their wife or other family names attached.

3.  If you’re Michael O’Hare, once you’re safely ensconced at your new career location, you write an op-ed piece that attacks your fake antagonist (Gareth Penn) while being careful not to mention or refer to your actual antagonist (Ray Grant) since, if you do make the mistake of referring to him in any way, he can then sue YOU and demand your fingerprints.

It’s a very simple idea.  Even most pro wrestling fans get it.  But not Zodiac message board posters.  So I’ll ask them this question:
