



“Radian”



The Zodiac concludes his letter of 26 July 1970 with this cryptic postscript:

PS. The Mt. Diablo code concerns radians & # inches along the radians.

It appears to refer to his mailing of 29 June 1970, which contains the fourth cipher (presumably the “code”) and an annotated gas station road map on which he has drawn a clock face the center of whose dial is Mount Diablo in Contra Costa County.

Whatever the postscript means — or even if it is meaningless — it contains the word “radian,” which seems out of place in the vocabulary of a serial murderer who had been pigeonholed as a semi-moronic high school dropout (e.g. in Professor Murray Miron’s psychological profile, which describes him as no more than high school educated).

The radian is a unit of angular measurement generally used by engineers and other professionals who find it, for various reasons, to be more useful than other units (degrees or mils*). It is defined as an angle subtended by an arc equal in length to the radius of the circle of which it is a part. This means that there are 2π (6.283185…) radians to the circle; thus, it can also be defined as a circle divided by 2π. A third way to define it is to translate it into the more familiar degree; there are 57.2957795131…° in a radian. In any case, the radian is familiar to people who have studied mathematics at the college level, but high school dropouts, not so much.
[*The mil is defined as 1/6400 of a circle; it is used by the artillery branch of all NATO armies and probably others as well. The degree is divided into minutes and the minute into seconds, so fire direction using degrees would require two stages of unit conversion in an environment requiring rapid computation. The civilian analogy would be trying to figure out how much to tip the waiter using pre-Decimalization British currency while you’re rushing to catch your train and your girlfriend is nagging you. Having to convert from one unit to another — seconds to minutes, for instance — not only takes more time but also increases the likelihood of computation error, which can be fatal on the battlefield. For all practical purposes, the mil works well as is, and in a pinch, you can interpolate a half-mil.]

Between the summer of 1970 and November 1981, the contents of this letter had been withheld from the public (along with all but a fraction of the other letters), and the Zodiac’s use of the word “radian” was not publicly known.

My father, Hugh Penn, was employed as a statistician by the California Department of Justice in Sacramento. His office was located a couple of doors away from the office of the person who was nominally in charge of the department’s Zodiac investigation — but in practice was the custodian of several filing cabinets filled with documents from the local police agencies that had active Zodiac cases — and my father told me that he seemed to spend most of his time at work doing crossword puzzles.

Dad had been stationed at Henderson Field on Guadalcanal during the war, functioning as a cryptographer in a USAAF communications unit. He found his job boring, so he amused himself by publishing a unit newspaper titled The SNAFU Review. His training had given him an introduction to the basic principles of cryptology, and he had supplemented his military training by reading Fletcher Pratt’s Secret and urgent, a popular work on cryptology.

Since the Zodiac had baffled the world with his ciphers, Dad asked his Zodiac co-worker if he might read the unpublished Zodiac letters, photographic copies of which were in the possession of all law enforcement agencies with a Zodiac case; his ambition was to solve the unsolved ciphers. He had taken an abortive stab at business shortly before my birth; after a stint as a staff writer for a San Jose newspaper and his time in the Army Air Forces, he opted for the safety and security of government employment. But years of work for the state left him feeling unfulfilled; he chafed at office routine and thought that solving the Zodiac puzzle would enable him to break out of his rut.

Back in Sacramento, Dad asked his Zodiac co-worker if he could try his hand at solving the ciphers, and he looked up, said okay with a shrug, then went back to his crossword puzzle, probably thinking that at worst, Dad couldn’t do any damage. But after many hours of attempted analysis, he had gotten nowhere, so he concluded that they were gibberish and consequently unsolvable. He read all the letters but made no attempt to analyze them, not considering that they — or portions of them, anyway — might be encrypted. The word “radian” in the July 1970 letter did, however, strike him as incongruous, since it was out of place in the lexicon of the type of person the Zodiac was supposed to be. The contents of this letter had been kept hidden from the public, so he was one of only a handful of individuals who knew about the Zodiac’s use of the word “radian.” (By “handful,” I mean no more than ten people.)

In late 1980, he told me about it, and it gave me the idea that this incongruity might be the key to solving the puzzle. You might say that I inherited his ambition. “Radian” is mathematical terminology; the Zodiac letters might contain the answers that everyone was looking for in the ciphers when looked at in the light of mathematics. This led to the formulation of a theory that I don’t want to go into because in hindsight, it isn’t worth discussing. But it seemed important at the time, and I took it to the Napa Sheriff’s Department, which had jurisdiction over the Lake Berryessa murder. The original investigator of that murder, Captain Kenneth Narlow, was still on the job. His counterparts in other departments had all retired.

In late December 1980, I barged into his office and plonked my half-baked theory down on his desk. He seemed to find it interesting, listening attentively to my presentation. He asked me to define “radian,” and I did it three different ways (see above). He listened intently, then said, “You know, for the last ten and a half years, I’ve wondered what that word meant.” If I had been standing on the rim of the Grand Canyon, I would never have seen my lower jaw again. To say that I was astounded would have been the understatement of the century, but I did my best to conceal my astonishment. It seemed to me that a murderer’s use of specialized technical vocabulary ought to have provided an insight, if not into his identity, at least into what kind of person he was, a category into which he could be placed. Narlow had interviewed over 900 people who had briefly been what they call “persons of interest” at one time or another. How much work might he have spared himself if had concentrated his investigative effort on individuals who had a certain kind of job or educational background? In any case, it seemed to me to be tantamount to dereliction of duty not to find out in 1970 what “radian” meant and wait until some random walk-in enlightened him ten years later.

Bob Graysmith, who wrote the first Zodiac book (Zodiac; St. Martin’s Press, 1986), is responsible for turning the Zodiac murders into a national and ultimately international celebrity case. Before then, it was virtually unknown outside of California, and mainly in the San Francisco Bay Area at that. Graysmith had been employed as editorial cartoonist by the San Francisco Chronicle and had made use of his job there to snag photocopies of all the letters that were sent to the paper. He deserves credit for publishing them in his book; they would otherwise have remained hidden from public view for years longer. But otherwise, I don’t have anything good to say about Zodiac. For instance, he quotes the Mount Diablo postscript but then does not explain to his readers, most of whom do not have a math background, what “radian” means. The word just lies there on the page. As an author, he was derelict in his duty to his reader. He theorizes endlessly about everything else* but doesn’t utter a peep about this peculiar word.
[*In his discussion of the Bus Bomb Letter of 20 April 1970, Graysmith glosses the Zodiac’s “cerous” as a misspelling of “curious.” I feel that most readers don’t need his help in figuring that out. What they do need is a definition. If I were writing a book about the Hudson’s Bay Company and used the word “plew,” I would feel obligated to let my readers know that it is a Canadian word for “beaver pelt.” In Graysmith’s place, I would have added that “cerous” is a real English word: pertaining to the natural element cerium in an oxidation state of +3.]

In the meantime, I had beaten Graysmith into print with an article in the November 1981 issue of California magazine. I don’t recommend it — it’s the Model T of my product line — but it does set forth three important things: 1) the Zodiac’s odd use of “radian;” 2) his conversion of English to number by way of Morse Code and base-two arithmetic; and 3) the division of the Zodiac’s first — solved — cipher into 390 letters of English and 18 letters of gibberish as an expression of the mathematical constant π: 390 : 18 :: 3 : 0.14159…. Both the integer and fraction have been multiplied by 130 (3 x 130 = 390; 0.14159… x 130 = 18.4).

One of the advantages of Morse Code as a means of expression for someone who wants to conceal his meaning is that Morse Code is digital, and a digital system makes it possible to redivide words and names so that they become completely unrecognizable. Redivision means altering a string of characters by inserting, deleting, or shifting spaces between them. For instance, redivision transforms SUPER BOWL into SUPERB OWL and THERAPIST into THE RAPIST; the single word MENDACITY becomes a sentence: MEND A CITY. Not only does redivision change meaning; it alters pronunciation as well. On the digital level, it can create and destroy letters. For instance, the word THREE is Morse 1 0000 010 0 0; redivision enables it to morph into BUS: 1000 001 000. These two words do not have a single letter in common, but both express the same base-two number (520).

A number of people who knew about my theory told me that they could easily write a program to translate alphanumeric texts into Morse Code and vice versa, but after I suggested that they do so, I never heard from them again. Eventually, it occurred to me that if the texts were digitized in a conventional word processing application, they could be searched by the search and replace feature for any particular digital string. I used Word, and in days of yore when I still had a computer that would support Word 5.1, I could search not only individual texts but the entire folder of digitized Zodiac texts all at once.

The probability of the random occurrence at any given place of a single Morse digit (1 or 0) is 1/21 (1/2), the probability of a two-digit string (10, 11, 01, 00) is 1/22 (1/4). A thirteen-digit string has a probability of 1/213, or 1/8192, and so forth. The fewer times a redivision appears, the more valuable it is; a drawback of the Zodiac’s system is a high noise-to-signal ratio, and rarity enhances the signal. A single occurrence in a body of literature is known by the technical term hapax legomenon (Greek for “a thing said once”). Here’s an example. There is only one letter in which the Zodiac explicitly mentions the Riverside murder (Los Angeles Times Letter, 15 March 1971). He writes not only the explicit word RIVERSIDE but also a hapax-redivision of the same place name (RIVERSIDE = 010 00 0001 0 010 000 00 100 0; [DOW]NTHERETHERE[ASON] = 10 1 0000 0 010 0 1 0000 0 010 0 01). Since RIVERSIDE is written in twenty-two Morse digits, the probability of its random occurrence is 1/222 or 1/4,194,304. The fact that the only text in which its redivision occurs is also the only one whose subject is Riverside makes a random occurrence even less likely. And all doubt about its being the product of design is dispelled by geometry. When the text is formatted in the same format used by the Zodiac for his ciphers (17-column rectangle), literal RIVERSIDE and its digital redivision define a 6 x 11 rectangle expressing the year of the Riverside murder (66). This letter deserves a geographic footnote. All but one of the Zodiac letters in the period December 1968-March 1971 are postmarked in San Francisco; this one is the exception. It is postmarked in Pleasanton (Alameda County). The San Francisco postmarks suggest that that city was his base. Dropping a letter in Pleasanton required an eighty-mile round trip from San Francisco involving the expenditure of time, effort, gasoline money, wear and tear on his car, and bridge tolls. The Zodiac went considerably out of his way to obtain a Pleasanton postmark; there must have been a payoff of some kind. It seems obvious to me if no one else that he went to all that trouble because Pleasanton had a single ZIP Code: 94566. The last two digits express the year of the Riverside murder. There may have been a 94166 ZIP Code in San Francisco, but San Francisco postmarks gave only the first three digits (941-). This is just one of a number of nonverbal hints he dropped about his authorship of the Riverside murder: the Phillips 66 road map in the June 1970 mailing and the use of two six-cent stamps on several envelopes (when one would do) also alluded to Riverside. 

In the first cipher — solved within days of receipt — the Zodiac writes WHEN I DIE I WILL BE REBORN…. Some of the speculation surrounding the Zodiac theorizes that the author is an adherent of an — always unspecified — Oriental religion whose fundamental principle is reincarnation. I suggest reading it instead as a mathematical statement. A life that begins with death is circular. The circle is a geometric figure which begins where it ends. An everyday example is the clock: not only is midnight the end of the day; it is also the beginning.

One of the three definitions I gave Ken Narlow was that a radian is a unit of angular measurement of which there are 2π to the circle, or 6.283185… (3.14159265… x 2). Anything circular must therefore contain 6.283185… radians. A computer search of the Zodiac texts shows that in addition to the two literal RADIANs in the July 1970 letter, there are four hidden digital redivisions of the word. The particulars are shown in the exhibit below. It takes six complete radians and 0.283185… of a seventh to make a circle. The literature contains six RADIANs, all right. But what about the fraction (2π-6 or 0.28315…)?

Before I get to that, let me mention that the literature contains only one redivision of TWO PI, the total number of radians in a circle. It appears at the end of the literature, in the letter of July 1974 in which the Zodiac complains about the Chronicle columnist Count Marco. Or at least, that’s what the letter seems to be about. The author uses the phrase FROM WHENCE, Morse 0010 010 111 11 011 0000 0 10 1010 0. That contains this redivision: 1 011 111 0110 00 (TWO PI); ROMWH = 010 111 11 011 0000.
Examples are shown in the same 17-column rectangular format as the Zodiac ciphers, two of which are perfect n x 17 rectangles (24 x 17, 20 x 17). The third is 13 characters and so does not appear to have a format, but the fourth is 32 characters in one row of 17 and a second of 15; it is therefore an imperfect 2 x 17 rectangle. Since the format is reflected in three of the four ciphers, it seems reasonable to see the third as an imperfect example of the same format as well.

The word RADIAN in Morse Code (010 01 100 00 01 10) expresses the base-two number 4870; 4870 x 0.283185… = 1379.1119…. The text containing RADIAN #6 is postmarked in early October 1970; seven more texts were mailed following that, from late October 1970 to July 1974. The contents of those texts are presented here as a consolidated unit and in 50-character rows to facilitate counting. The difference between the initial letter of RADIAN #6 and the initial letter of TWO PI = 1380 alphanumeric characters. I can offer two explanations for the difference. One is that #1380 contains the fraction 0.1119…; in mathematical terminology, the fraction slops over into the next higher integer. The other is that 1380 is, as they say, close enough for government work. Here’s a comparison:

0.283185 2π-6
0.283162 1379/4870
0.283367 1380/4870

For all practical purposes, the difference is negligible. It is more of a stretch to see the same thing expressed in the difference in time between the postmarks of the texts containing RADIAN #6 (6 October 1970) and TWO PI (8 July 1974). It’s 1371 days, which is still suspiciously close:

0.281519 1371/4870
0.283185 2π-6

Two and a half millennia ago, Archimedes estimated the value of π to be 22/7, and for centuries, it served well. Here’s a comparison of the above two values and 2π-6 according to Archimedes:

0.281519 1371/4870
0.283185 2π-6
0.285714 Archimedes

The Zodiac’s 1371/4870 is substantially closer to the established value than Archimedes is.

Ken Narlow was surprised to learn that the fraction of π (π–3) is endless; I think I used 0.14159 in my presentation to him. He told me that he had been taught in school that π = 3.1. Most people are satisfied with 3.14. Because of its resemblance to π, 14 March (3/14) is popularly known as Pi Day. On 14 March 2020, I purchased groceries at the Lake City Fred Meyer supermarket. I noticed that the baked goods department was having a sale on fruit pies in honor of Pi Day. A blueberry pie that ordinarily goes for $4.99 was to be had at a special sale price of $3.14. I bought two, since $6.28 is the number of radians in a circle.

No matter how far you carry out the fraction, it will always be inexact. It goes on endlessly, forever. How far you carry it out is dictated by need. If you need a circular part for a Mars rover, you would want the CNC machinist fabricating it to use the closest approximation of π-3 his milling machine is capable of;* but if you are cutting a circular hole in the door of an outhouse, 3.1 would get the job done; all Ken Narlow needed to graduate from high school was that one decimal place. Fred Meyer is happy with two decimal places (π = 3.14). The 1380-character difference between the Zodiac’s RADIAN #6 and TWO PI yields a value of 2π that is accurate to three decimal places (2π = 6.283). The difference in time between the Hole Card (containing RADIAN #6) and the Count Marco Letter (TWO PI) is 1371 days, which gives you the same degree of accuracy needed by the New York Times to publish a π-themed crossword puzzle on the 14th day of March. (I go on at such length about precision because I anticipate a torrent of cavils.)
[*In November 1962, I attended a conference in Bad Godesberg, where I met Lockheed’s liaison to the German factory building the F-104G fighter under license from Lockheed. Over dinner, he told me that the American version was designed using traditional units — feet and inches — but the German version was built using machine tools calibrated in metric units — meters and fractions of meters. Machine tools calibrated in one system can match the accuracy of machine tools calibrated in the other to only so many decimal places; no matter how far you carry out traditional and metric fractions, they will never be precisely alike. The cumulative effect of hundreds of tiny differences had necessitated a complete reworking of the aircraft. This may have had something to do with the sad history of the F-104G, which became notorious as a widow-maker.] 

Why didn’t the Zodiac mail the Count Marco Letter eight days later to make the fraction more precise? One possible answer is that he didn’t need to; 1371 days was accurate enough (see above). Another is that a later postmark might have conflicted with other design criteria, such as expressive postmarks.* A third is that it might have been inconvenient, if, say, he was not a resident of the Bay Area but commuted from elsewhere; he might not have been able to reconcile his Zodiac letter mailing-schedule with his employer’s or family’s convenience. In any case, it’s close enough.
[*I will discuss expressive postmarks in an appendix.] 

When you express an angular measurement in radians, it is convenient to have some kind of brief notation to go with the number. For instance, when you do this in degrees, you have special symbol to identify the number as a number of degrees (°): a circle = 360°. For the U.S. Army artillery, a circle = 6400 mil. The abbreviation denoting radians is rad; a circle = 2πrad. How many RADs are there in the Zodiac literature? The answer is six and a fraction. There is no way to express an irrational fraction using only three Roman letters. Any fraction will have to do. There are seven RADs in the literature, the last of which is found on the back of the Hallowe’en Card, where it appears as part of the word PARADICE written vertically; SLAVES crosses PARADICE in the horizontal, and the two words share the letter A. Since it is part of two words, it seems fair to see the A of RAD as half of A (A/2); this RAD is therefore as not 1 rad but 0.8333… rad (2.5/3 = 0.8333…). “Close enough for government work” can’t be stretched to close the gap between 0.283185 and 0.833333, but if you start with “a circle contains six radians and a fraction of a seventh,” then the Zodiac’s treatment of RAD gets the job done: 0.833… is indeed a fraction.

Four of the six RADs appear in texts postmarked in July, as do the explicit RADIANs and the lone TWO PI-redivision. I suggest this is not fortuitous. The number of degrees in a radian is 57.295779…. The word JULY, written in Morse Code and with a decimal point between U and L, expresses this value to eight decimal places (0111 001 . 0100 1011 = 57.292968…). The dates JU.LY1, JU.LY2, JU.LY5, JU.LY11, and JU.LY23 replicate exactly, digit for digit, the base-two writing of 57.299578…, the last to thirteen decimal places: JU.LY23 (0111 001 . 0100 1011 10111) = 57.295776 (accurate to five places). Let’s suppose that in the Zodiac’s scheme, 23 July, the closest approximation of the radian’s degree-value = 1 rad. Then in the word PARADICE (used three times in the literature), if RAD = 23 July, then PAR = RAD-2, or 21 July. Morse PAR (0110 01 010) is base-two 202, and 21 July is, in a common year, the 202nd day. What a coincidence.

Elsewhere, I point out that the Zodiac’s murder of a taxicab driver (11 October 1969) is an allusion to another specialized mathematical term that most people are not familiar with: the taxicab number. It is defined as the smallest number expressing the sum of two cubes n different ways where n = the number of ways in which it is arrived at. There are to date only six known taxicab numbers. The number 1729 is the classic taxicab number, partly because it was the first one in the category and partly because it was the identifying number of an actual London taxicab. It is the sum of 13 + 123 and 93 + 103. At the time of the Zodiac murders, only three were known: 2, 1729, and 87,539,319. Here’s a manifestation in a Zodiac text. The array of RADIANs and TWO PI encompasses the entire body of Zodiac letters, from first to last. The first RADIAN occurs in his first letter (29 November 1966) and TWO PI in the last (8 July 1974). From the beginning of the Zodiac episode — the murder of Cheri Jo Bates in Riverside on 30 October 1966 — to the postmark on the Hallowe’en Card containing the fractional RAD, 1458 days elapse. I doesn’t seem likely to be fortuitous that that is the sum of two cubes (1458 = 93 + 93).




Appendix A

The circularity of life


The notion that life is circular is not just a casual affectation. It is rooted in the Zodiac’s system of converting English to number by way of Morse Code and base-two number. This system yields a number of startling coincidences that, while intrinsically meaningless, might appear, to a receptive mind, to be the keystone of the universe. In reality, they are dictated by the peculiarities of English spelling, the symbols chosen by Samuel F. B. Morse to represent the letters of the alphabet, and the rules for writing base-two numbers. They would not produce the same results in a different language, so there is no way they could be universal. Even so, it’s hard not to feel the seductive pull of a breathtaking coincidence. The important thing here is how seriously the Zodiac takes it.

Birth is the beginning of life and the end is, well, the end. BIRTH is Morse 1000 00 010 1 0000, base-two 8272; THE END is 1 0000 0 0 10 100, base-two 2068. They are redivisions of one another. BIRTH is essentially just THE END with two additional zeros (BIRTH = THE END x 22); they are fundamentally related to one another. Stripped of all postpositive zeros, they both yield the number 517 (8272 x 2-4; 2068 x 2-2). LIFE is Morse 0100 00 0010 0, base-two 516. What follows LIFE but 517, the skeleton of both BIRTH and THE END?

Marshall McLuhan’s book Understanding media appeared in 1964; the only passage from it that anyone remembers today is “the medium is the message.” Great minds think alike; whether he had read McLuhan or not, the Zodiac dropped a hint about the circular nature of life in his first written text, not a letter but an inscription scratched into a desktop at the Riverside City College library, from whose parking lot he abducted his first victim. The desk was the medium. Morse DESK (100 0 000 101) is base-two 517. The desktop inscription, by the way, is the only Zodiac text containing both literal LIFE and literal DEATH.

The four ciphers are written in 408, 340, 13, and 32 characters, respectively, for a total of 793. They exhibit the 17-column rectangular format. When they are consolidated into a unitary text of the same format, they form a rectangle of 46 complete rows and an incomplete 47th row of 11 characters: 793 = (46 x 17) + 11. The first and last symbols, the beginning and the end (##1 and 793) are the same: an open triangle; and they define an 11 x 47 rectangle expressing a product of 517.

A timepiece, whether clock or watch,* has a circular dial. The hands are anchored at the center of the dial, describing a circle as they sweep its circumference. The center of CLOCK (O) is position #2068, the digital spelling of THE END (1 0000 0 0 10 100 = 2068). The center of WATCH (T) is #268, the digital spelling of DEAD (100 0 01 100 = 268). The minute is just another unit of circular measurement; instead of 360 degrees, or 6400 mils, or 2π radians, the hour hand divides the circle into 720 minutes and the minute hand into 60. Essentially, the hour hand is all you need to tell the time; it points to the exact hour and minute, and so the first watches had no minute hand. But most people don’t have the visual acuity it takes to read the exact position of the hour hand on a small dial; the minute hand was added to make that easier. When THE END and DEAD are written as angular measurements, they are identical: 2068° (THE END°) = 268° (DEAD°). The difference is 1800° (2068° - 268°); 360° = 0°; therefore, 1800° (360° x 5) = 0°: 268° + (360° x 5) is another way of saying 268° + 0°. The difference between DEAD and THE END is zero.
[*On a linguistic note, English appears to be the only language that makes a distinction between clocks and watches. Other languages use the same word to denote both; I have never been able to discover an exception.]

The same information was available in July 1969, but it went unnoticed because it was nonverbal in nature. People may get some forms of nonverbal communication, e.g. so-called “body language,” tone of voice, or facial expressions, but they seem to have difficulty perceiving other manifestations, let alone verbalizing and analyzing them.

Be that as it may, the mailing of the first cipher on 31 July 1969 was the third event in a series. Appendix D

Criminal investigation and oil spills


On 18 January 1971, San Francisco Bay was covered with a blanket of thick white fog. An oil tanker laden to the Plimsoll line with crude oil was headed in through the Golden Gate, bound for a refinery in the Sacramento-San Joaquin river delta. Another, empty, tanker was outward bound. They collided in mid-Bay, causing a catastrophic oil spill. Fortunately, the tide was going out, so the crude oil was swept out to sea. Unfortunately, the tide came back in, and the oil was deposited on the ocean beaches of Marin and San Mateo Counties. The only good thing to come out of this disaster was that it inspired the wildlife biologists of Point Reyes Bird Observatory — Lynne Stenzel* in particular — to invent the beached-bird survey as a tool for assessing environmental damage. This method is now used worldwide. Otherwise, it was a wretched mess of huge proportions.
[*I worked at PRBO for ten years and so knew Lynne. Once I asked her what she and her husband were doing on the weekend; another mom might have said, “We’re taking the kids to Chuck E. The Coast Guard’s board of inquiry established that the master of neither vessel had been using radar to navigate the fogged-in waters of the Bay; they had relied instead on the naked eye. There was only one person who had been paying attention to radar data, a yeoman at the Coast Guard station on Yerba Buena Island. His radar screen displayed all the shipping on the Bay. The impending collision of the two tankers was apparent, but no one at the Coast Guard station had picked up the radiotelephone to notify the officers on the bridges of the two vessels that one of them was about to slice open the hull of the other. The reason for this failure to communicate and thus avert environmental disaster was that there was no procedure for doing so, no rule in the rulebook saying “If you see that something horrible is about to happen, do something to prevent it.” In other words, if your mommy doesn’t explicitly say, “Don’t stuff beans into your nostrils,” you are free to pack your air passages with dried legumes.

Criminal investigators have a manual, written or unwritten, that prescribes standard ways of conducting an investigation. There is nothing in the police manual that says “If you come across an unusual word in the writings of a serial murderer, find out what the word means.” This is why Ken Narlow spent ten years diligently avoiding dictionaries, which are not conventional sources of evidence. In 1987, I got a gratuitous public lecture from a prosecutor in Suffolk County, Massachusetts, informing me that he didn’t use mathematics in murder investigations. I agree — to a point; but most rules come with exceptions: the pitcher goes to the well until it breaks. Neither dictionaries nor math — nor anything else — should be excluded without exception, because a case may arise in which either or both will be appropriate. The Zodiac’s use of the word “radian” suggested to me alone of all the people in the world that his murders are the exception. I feel that the presentation above proves me right.
