The cipher

  • Unit = one column of three cells (top, middle, bottom). Each cell is empty (0), a diagonal stroke (1), or a square (2).
  • Read it as a three-digit base-3 number with the bottom cell as the leading digit: value = 9×bottom + 3×middle + top. Values 1–26 are A–Z; the empty column 000 (value 0) is a word space. All 27 combinations are valid, so every possible glyph decodes to something.
  • Red scale: a square is a hollow box, a stroke is a short \ across the cell’s diagonal. Strokes in diagonal neighbours join into one long line.
  • Black scale (double size): a square is a solid block, a stroke is two small squares touching corner-to-corner (the “checkerboard” look). Glyphs are three big cells tall.
  • Not in the code: the four tall red bars (sentence marks, written / above) and the three handwritten !.

Worked example: M = 13 = 111 (stroke in all three rows, a zig-zag chain in black); I = 9 = 100 (stroke in the bottom row only). So the very first black glyph, a short pair plus a chain, is IM. S = 19 = 201 (bottom square, top stroke); K = 11 = 102 (bottom stroke, top square).

Encode / decode tool

presets:

Letters A–Z (any case), spaces (→ 000), / or | (bar), ! (literal). Apostrophes are dropped like the writer did; any other character is skipped and reported.

examples:

Separate units with spaces or commas; 000 is a space, / a bar. In numeral mode a run of digits is split into groups of three. In symbol mode . (or ·) is empty, \ (or ╲) a stroke, # (or □) a square, three characters per unit, top first.

Notes & checks

The S in BROS

The fourth unit of the bottom block’s fifth glyph is drawn as S: a top stroke (two small squares at top-left and bottom-right of the top cell) and a solid bottom block (201). The same unit with the two shapes exchanged – top square, bottom stroke – is K (102). That makes BROK, the start of BROKE, which also fits the story (“the one who broke the mirror”). It would be a single mix-up of which shape goes where; the glyph has no E after it. As drawn it reads BROS, which I show unchanged (dashed red unit in the board).

Other conventions

  • Apostrophe: not encoded (IM twice in each scale).
  • Bars: four tall red bars, after YEAH, TITLE, SPEED and YOU. They are as tall as a column but not a cell pattern in the code, so I treat them as sentence marks (/ in the plaintext).
  • Spaces: a blank column (000) is my reading of the word gaps; the code has 27 values and the blank is the natural 27th.
  • BORED is split across two black clusters; READ and THIS each have a stray lone mark that belongs to the next/previous column (drawing artefacts, they decode correctly as columns).
  • Glare: a patch on line 2 hides the unit(s) of IT (“LIKED IT”); they are read from context.

How it was found

  1. Geometry. Edge-sharing hollow squares sit 37–58 px apart (about 46 px); a lone stroke is 57–73 px long ≈ 46√2, i.e. the diagonal of one cell. Rasterising each red glyph onto that lattice gave at most three rows for every glyph but one (a fit error), and hand-drawn repeats came out as identical matrices.
  2. Base 3. Reading each three-cell column as a ternary number (hint from the researcher: “each red glyph looks like base 3”) and trying 48 natural digit/direction/alphabet readings, one produced English: 5 exact common words in the raw red text against a maximum of 4 in 5,000 random mappings (P < 0.0002 for the chosen mapping; < 0.01 after allowing for the 48 readings tried).
  3. Same code at double scale. A 2×2 block of marker squares is a square, a corner-touching pair is a stroke. With the mapping fixed beforehand the black top block gave 4 exact common words (random maximum 2, P < 0.0002) and, re-read by eye, the sentence IM BORED AND HAVE STUFF TO DO; the repeated T-shaped glyphs are THE.
  4. Complete plaintext. The full red text and the bottom block were completed by the researcher; the table above regenerates the board from it, and the check above compares it with the photo.
